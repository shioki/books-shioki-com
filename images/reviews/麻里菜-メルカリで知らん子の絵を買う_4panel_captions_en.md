### Panel 1
**Scene**: At dusk in an Edo-period alley, a young townsgirl in a kimono strolls past a small market stall selling children’s strange doodles. She pauses, puzzled yet intrigued, wondering why anyone would buy a stranger’s child’s drawing. The atmosphere blends quiet curiosity with the gentle bustle of daily life — wooden houses, lantern light, and people in kimono passing by.  
**Dialogue**: (Thinking) “Why would someone treasure a child’s scribble?”

### Panel 2
**Scene**: In her small study lit by a flickering candle, the townsgirl reads a Dutch book about curiosity and creativity beside her scrolls of Japanese mathematics. On the wall hangs a portrait of a modern-looking woman with short hair and calm, analytical eyes — humorous yet thoughtful. The portrait seems to glance at her, whispering softly, “Uselessness is freedom.”  
**Dialogue**: (Whisper from the portrait) “Uselessness is freedom.”

### Panel 3
**Scene**: Inspired, the townsgirl sketches absurd inventions — a teapot with legs, a poetry-writing fan — and laughs at her own nonsense. She hangs one of her whimsical drawings outside her home. A curious child stops to look and smiles. The scene is lively and humorous, celebrating the joy of embracing the unnecessary.  
**Dialogue**: (Townsgirl, laughing) “How delightfully pointless!”

### Panel 4
**Scene**: Night falls. Sitting by the window, the townsgirl gazes at the moonlight glimmering on her brush and inkstone. She writes a tanka on a strip of paper, capturing the essence of her reflection — serene, tender, and quietly radiant.  
**Dialogue**: (Softly) “Even what seems useless can move the heart.”  
**Tanka (translation)**:  
Even useless things—  
pillars of life where light falls;  
a stranger’s child’s art  
stirs something deep within me,  
a quiet motion of the heart.  
**Tanka (original)**:  
むだごとも  
いのちのはしら  
光さす  
知らぬ子の絵に  
心うごけり
