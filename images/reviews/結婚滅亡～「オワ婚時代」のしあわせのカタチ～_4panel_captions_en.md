### Panel 1
**Scene**: On a lively Edo street lined with nagaya row houses and a nearby terakoya, the townsgirl pauses after overhearing people say, “Of course everyone must marry.” Holding a small shopping basket and a folded tanzaku, she looks thoughtful and slightly skeptical. Around her, she notices different ways of living—a cheerful single artisan, a gossiping married couple, a busy widow, and friends sharing food. The scene quietly questions whether marriage is truly the only natural path.  
**Dialogue**: (none; her expression conveys curiosity and quiet rebellion)

### Panel 2
**Scene**: In a cluttered study corner lit by a paper lantern, she eagerly reads a wasan arithmetic book and a Dutch studies text. Instead of drawing hearts, she sketches diagrams of linked circles and broken bridges, realizing that marriage was often upheld by social systems and matchmaking networks rather than pure affection. Beside her appears an imagined scholar—middle-aged, calm, with tied-back hair and a plain kimono—pointing to a chart showing how omiai and communal ties once connected people. The atmosphere is one of discovery and analytical insight.  
**Dialogue**: (none; the mentor’s gesture and her focused gaze express understanding)

### Panel 3
**Scene**: From a high angle, the townsgirl energetically builds an “Edo connection map” in a communal alley using abacus beads, strings, and wooden tags to link neighbors through hobbies, study circles, shared meals, poetry, and trade instead of marriage alone. Townspeople gather in surprise—a fish seller joins a reading group, an elderly woman offers childcare help, a craftsman shares dinner, and a solitary diner smiles. The web of ties spreads across the alley like a living network. The humor lies in her treating human relationships as mathematical and scientific diagrams, proving that happiness can grow through many flexible bonds.  
**Dialogue**: (none; laughter and surprise fill the scene)

### Panel 4
**Scene**: At dusk, by a wooden veranda or paper window, the townsgirl writes a waka on a tanzaku with calm satisfaction. The soft ink tones and open space evoke mono no aware—a quiet awareness of impermanence and connection.  
**Dialogue**: (the waka appears written on the tanzaku)  
**Tanka (translation)**:  
Marriage is not  
the only form of life—  
it was never so.  
The ties we weave between hearts  
are what nurture happiness.  
**Tanka (original)**:  
みな婚は  
つねの姿に  
あらざりき  
つなぐ縁こそ  
幸を育てる
