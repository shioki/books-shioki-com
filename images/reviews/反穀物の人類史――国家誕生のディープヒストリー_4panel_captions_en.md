### Panel 1
**Scene**: On a bustling Edo street lined with wooden row houses and a temple school, the young townsgirl pauses to watch officials and merchants measuring and recording sacks of rice. Her curious yet uneasy expression reflects a realization: what can be easily counted can also be easily controlled. Around her, townsfolk trade vegetables, fish, and gathered foods—abundant but less traceable to authority. The atmosphere hints at her question: is settled life and grain truly progress, or merely a tool for governance?  
**Dialogue**: (none)

### Panel 2
**Scene**: In a candlelit study filled with wasan scrolls, abacuses, Dutch books, maps, and botanical sketches, the townsgirl studies intently, connecting ideas with ink notes and diagrams. She compares the richness of marshland life, pre-agricultural living, delayed state formation, and the governability of grain. Nearby appear two scholarly presences: a calm, intellectual mentor depicted as a hanging scroll portrait or ghostly companion with round spectacles and a thoughtful gaze, and a composed Japanese translator-scholar in a simple kimono working by a manuscript. Their presence feels like quiet guidance from learned spirits.  
**Dialogue**: (none)

### Panel 3
**Scene**: In a lively village courtyard, the townsgirl performs a playful demonstration. On one side, she neatly stacks and counts rice bundles; on the other, fish, nuts, roots, reeds, and shellfish spill and scatter beyond easy accounting. She wields an abacus dramatically, dodging geese and gesturing with a writing brush to show that grain is not merely food but a ruler’s dream—visible, measurable, taxable, and seizable. A stern local official eagerly counts rice but looks bewildered by the unruly abundance of wild foods. Her expression glows with both humor and revelation.  
**Dialogue**: (none)

### Panel 4
**Scene**: At dusk by a quiet riverbank or on an engawa veranda, the townsgirl writes a tanka on a slender tanzaku card. The scene is serene, with soft ink tones and open space. Her expression is calm yet resonant, suggesting reflection on freedom beyond the reach of authority. The moment conveys that agriculture and statehood are not the inevitable peak of civilization, and that life beyond legibility may preserve autonomy.  
**Dialogue**: (none)  
**Tanka (translation)**:  
Before the grain,  
there were homes already rooted—  
a life begun.  
Nations bind themselves  
to what they can see ripen.

穀よりも
