### Panel 1
**Scene**: The Edo townsgirl stands in a lively marketplace, observing merchants as they mechanically go about their tasks. She tilts her head, feeling that something is absent — “Everyone works so hard, but… what is it all for?” The background depicts the bustling street life of Edo, with lanterns and a distant temple bell.  
**Dialogue**: "Everyone works so hard, but… what is it all for?"

### Panel 2
**Scene**: In her dimly lit study room, illuminated by candlelight, the townsgirl eagerly reads a foreign-style bound book titled "The New Type of Writing." A calm, thoughtful scholar resembling the author Yamaguchi Shu appears beside her, embodying an imagined Edo-era mentor. He has a composed expression, short hair neatly tied, and wears a simple haori, radiating quiet intellectual energy. He gestures toward the book, explaining the importance of “finding meaning beyond answers.”  
**Dialogue**: "It's about finding meaning beyond answers."

### Panel 3
**Scene**: The townsgirl is now teaching children at a terakoya, but instead of traditional arithmetic drills, she asks them, “Why do you want to learn numbers?” The children look confused at first, but then they smile as they start to imagine and draw their dreams. Ink brush motion lines convey a sudden warmth and spontaneity, hinting at a creative “play” that fills the room.  
**Dialogue**: "Why do you want to learn numbers?"

### Panel 4
**Scene**: In the evening, the townsgirl sits beside a paper lantern, gazing at the moon's reflection on the inkstone. She writes a waka on a small tanzaku, her expression serene and filled with quiet conviction.  
**Tanka (translation)**: "One should live by finding meaning rather than just correct answers, letting the heart wander in the moon's margins."  
**Tanka (original)**: 正解より　意味を見つけて　生きるべし  
月の余白に　心遊ばす
