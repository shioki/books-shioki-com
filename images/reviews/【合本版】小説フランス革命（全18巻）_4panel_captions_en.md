### Panel 1
**Scene**: In a busy Edo street lined with nagaya row houses and a terakoya, the townsgirl pauses as she overhears townspeople arguing over a new village rule and rice shortages. One fears punishment, another fears hunger, and another loudly invokes “justice,” yet their worried faces show that fear and self-protection drive them more than ideals. The heroine notices the contradiction, tilts her head in thought, and quietly senses that public opinion is not one single thing but many layered feelings. Period-accurate Edo details, posted notices fluttering in the wind, and a subtle tension running through ordinary daily life.  
**Dialogue**: “What if they punish us?” / “What if we run out of rice?” / “It’s justice, I tell you!”

### Panel 2
**Scene**: Inside a lamplit room lined with wasan scrolls, an abacus, and Dutch books, the townsgirl studies with intense focus, comparing numbers, notes, and maps as if trying to understand how societies move. A secondary figure representing the author appears as a mentor-like scholar or imagined portrait: a Japanese man in his late fifties, with short neat dark hair, no glasses, an average build, a calm intelligent expression, and a modest kimono, giving off a thoughtful, serious literary air. He points not to heroes but to tangled circles labeled “fear,” “procedure,” “power,” and “livelihood,” and she realizes that revolutions are shaped not only by belief, but by what people fear losing. Dynamic brush shadows, scholarly intimacy, and a strong feeling of emotional discovery.  
**Dialogue**: “Not only belief...” / “...but fear, procedure, power, and livelihood.”

### Panel 3
**Scene**: The heroine puts this insight to use in a lively and surprising street-side mediation scene. Two neighbors are quarreling fiercely over a missing bundle, each claiming noble righteousness. With comic boldness, she jumps into the middle, writes columns in the dirt with a brush, and uses an abacus to sort their motives: “fear of blame,” “fear of loss,” and “fear of officials.” Their anger collapses into embarrassed honesty. Use a dramatic overhead composition, sweeping motion lines, and humorous expressions. The visual idea suggests that facing a problem directly brings clarity, while avoidance only multiplies confusion.  
**Dialogue**: “I’m in the right!” / “No, I am!” / “Let’s sort this properly: fear of blame, fear of loss, fear of officials.”

The mood is reflective and poignant, with lingering shadows and the quiet atmosphere of an Edo evening.
