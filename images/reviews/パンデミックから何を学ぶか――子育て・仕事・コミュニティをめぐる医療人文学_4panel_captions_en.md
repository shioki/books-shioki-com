### Panel 1
**Scene**: On a narrow Edo street lined with wooden houses, the young townsgirl walks among uneasy townsfolk whispering about a mysterious epidemic in a nearby village. She pauses, watching their anxious faces, and quietly wonders if fear itself might be the deeper illness. A lantern in the background bears the faint word “care” (思いやり).  
**Dialogue**: *“Is fear the greater sickness?”*  

### Panel 2
**Scene**: That night, in her small study lit by a single candle, she opens a Dutch medical text and its handwritten translation, *On the Nature of Illness*. From the pages, two ethereal teachers appear — one a composed Western woman with gentle eyes and scholarly grace, the other a calm Japanese scholar with a thoughtful gaze. They speak across time, their words echoing softly.  
**Dialogue**: *“Illness is not the enemy, but a mirror to humanity.”*  

### Panel 3
**Scene**: The next morning, she visits a sick neighbor. Instead of stepping back in fear, she kneels beside them, offering warm tea and a kind ear. Her silhouette glows against the shōji’s filtered light — an image of empathy overcoming fear. Delicate sumi-e strokes hint at invisible threads connecting people, symbolizing community and coexistence.  
**Dialogue**: *“Let’s share warmth, not fear.”*  

### Panel 4
**Scene**: That evening, beneath the moonlight, she writes a tanka on a small scroll, her expression calm and illuminated with newfound understanding. The brushstrokes flow with quiet conviction.  
**Dialogue**: *(none)*  
**Tanka (translation)**:  
To live with illness—  
this too is the human path;  
beyond all our fear,  
we weave bonds together still,  
our hearts joined in compassion.  
**Tanka (original)**:  
病とも  
ともに生くるは  
人の道  
恐れを越えて  
絆を結ぶ
