### Panel 1
**Scene**: On a bustling Edo street near a temple school and rows of houses, a young townsgirl carrying groceries and notebooks pauses. She gazes up at the sky, then at the people around her—a priest, a merchant, a physician—each representing a different way of explaining the world. Her expression shows sudden curiosity and quiet existential wonder, as if asking herself, “Why does the world exist? Where do people come from and where do they go?”  
**Dialogue**: (None; her thoughts are conveyed through expression and atmosphere.)

### Panel 2
**Scene**: In a candlelit study filled with wasan scrolls, Dutch books, maps, and an abacus, the townsgirl studies intently. Strings and sketches connect ideas across religion, philosophy, and science. Beside her sits an elderly scholar with a calm, wise demeanor—her mentor—gesturing as he explains that knowledge arises from society, trade, translation, politics, and institutions, not from isolated genius. The mood is one of discovery and deep learning.  
**Dialogue**: Mentor (gently): “Ideas are born from the world we live in, not from solitude.”

### Panel 3
**Scene**: At a lively Edo market debate, townspeople argue over rumors, religion, and foreign knowledge. The townsgirl leaps into the center, holding an abacus in one hand and a brush in the other. Papers scatter as she urges everyone to question their assumptions before arguing. The crowd freezes in surprise as she “resolves” the conflict not with answers but with better questions. The composition is bold and dynamic, filled with expressive faces and humorous energy.  
**Dialogue**: Townsgirl (energetically): “Before we argue, shouldn’t we ask what we really know?”

### Panel 4
**Scene**: In the quiet evening by a paper window, the townsgirl sits with brush in hand, gazing at the moonlight and her open books. She writes a tanka on a tanzaku strip, reflecting that human thought through the ages is one long attempt to understand the world and ourselves.  
**Dialogue**: (She softly recites the poem as she writes.)  
**Tanka (translation)**:  
“When I ask the reason  
for the world’s existence,  
the answers divide—  
branching into religion,  
philosophy, and science.”  
**Tanka (original)**:  
世のわけを  
問えば答えは  
道わかれ  
宗教哲学  
科学へつづく
